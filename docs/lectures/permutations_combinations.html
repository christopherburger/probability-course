2: Permutations and Combinations

Traditional discussions in permutations and combinations focus on attempting to pattern-match word problems (order matters vs. order does not matter, with replacement and without replacement, etc.) instead of modeling state spaces. When we need to work with probabilistic algorithms, there are no word problems to pattern-match.

We will instead discuss permutations and combinations as concepts built to bypass the brute-force calculations of our previous state space creation.

Permutations

In our previous section, if you had 52 cards in a standard deck and drew 5, you would draw a tree with 52 branches, then 51 branches, then 50 branches, then 49 branches, then 48 branches.

That is infeasible, there is just too much to draw.

What we need is a way to truncate our multiplication tree based on prior information.

This is exactly what the idea of a permutation does. A permutation is a formula for a truncated multiplication tree.

We are just doing the multiplication principle, but stopping early. We write 52 \times 51 \times 50 \times 49 \times 48. This is reasonable since we have 52 choices for the first card, then 51 (as we have already chosen one card), then 50 (as we have chosen two), and so on. We are not concerned about the possibilities associated with the remaining 47 cards, as we are only drawing five.

We mathematically encode “stopping early” by writing the full tree (that is, every possible ordering which is 52!) and dividing off the branches we did not need (47!).

The general formula for a permutation is: nPk = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}

Where n is the total number of items and r is the number of items in out set of interest that were removed from the total.

But it is not a new concept; the formula comes just from being a typographical shortcut for multiplication. In a way, we can view the standard permutation formula as a form of compression for our multiplication tree.

Assume we are dealt three cards from a standard deck consisting of the Ace, King, and Queen of diamonds. What are all the permutations of how these cards could be dealt to us?

Combinations

Combinations are closely related to permutations and our goal is to think about them innately as a physical grouping mechanism whose purpose is to erase artificial distinctions.

Lets consider the practice problem above. We have three cards, the Ace, King, and Queen of diamonds. What if we are using them to determine a hand in a card game where order is not important? For example, a flush in poker is a hand that has all the same suit (in our case it is diamonds) and the hand is sized differently depending on the specifics of the game (it is usually five cards). Given this, does it matter what order we received the cards?

No, the order is no longer important here. The concept of a hand in poker makes no distinction between the physical arrangement of cards, they only must meet the much less restrictive requirement of being present in the hand.

For those familiar with the game of poker, you may say that a straight enforces an ordering of the cards. But this is only applicable to the entire hand. The dealer is not concerned if your hand is in ascending order, descending order or any possible ordering. If you have 5 sequentially ordered cards within your hand then regardless of their arrangement within your hand, you have a straight.

Since the physical reality of the game does not care about the order, we need a way to adjust our permutation formula to control for counting distinctions that do not matter. We saw that the permutation formula counted all 6 of those sequences as distinct branches on our tree.

A combination is an algorithm to bundle those 6 branches into a single “meta-branch.

If every valid state in our space has been artificially cloned exactly k! times by a permutation’s ordering, we divide the total state space by k! to collapse the clones into single entities.

\binom{n}{k} = nCk = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}

So in poker, the hand consisting of the Ace, King, and Queen of diamonds the number of combinations is \frac{3!}{3!(3-3)!} = 1 which matches our intuition with respect to the rules of poker.

For the sum over all i we have

\sum{i=0}{n} \binom{n}{i}\times 9^{n-i}

Which can be simplified using the Binomial Theorem to (9+1)^n = 10^n.

The Binomial Theorem

The physical intuition of the Binomial Theorem can be expressed as making an independent choice at every branch of a binary tree.

When you expand:

(x + y)^n = \underbrace{(x + y)(x + y)(x + y) \cdots (x + y)}_{n \text{ factors}}

You are just visiting n distinct locations or nodes in succession.

At each node, you must pick exactly one item: either an x or a y.

When you multiply out the brackets by the distributive law before collecting like terms, you form every possible sequence of n choices:

  • Pick x from all n boxes: you get x \cdot x \cdots x = x^n (only 1 way to do this).Pick y from Box 1, and x from the rest: you get y \cdot x \cdots x = yx^{n-1}.
  • Pick y from Box 2, and x from the rest: you get x \cdot y \cdot x \cdots x = xyx^{n-2}.

Because multiplication is commutative (yx = xy), every term that contains k copies of y and n-k copies of x collapses into the single algebraic monomial:

x^{n-k}y^k

Now ask the combinatorial question: How many distinct paths produce this exact monomial?

You have n nodes. You need to designate k of them from which to grab a y (the remaining n-k automatically supply an x). The number of ways to choose which k booths provide a y is, by definition: \binom{n}{k}

Thus, the coefficient of x^{n-k}y^k is simply the count of matching choice histories:

(x + y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} y^k

In the digit example, each position in an n-digit string faces a binary classification:

  • Option A: The digit is a non-zero (\{1, 2, \dots, 9\}) \rightarrow 9 possibilities.
  • Option B: The digit is a zero (\{0\}) \rightarrow 1 possibility.

The total number of unrestricted strings of length n is:

10^n = (9 + 1)^n

Now let x = 9 and y = 1:

(9 + 1)^n = \underbrace{(9 + 1)(9 + 1) \cdots (9 + 1)}_{n \text{ positions}}

At each digit position, you choose either one of the 9 non-zeros or the 1 zero:

To get exactly i zeros, you choose i factors to contribute the “1” (the zeros).The remaining n - i factors must contribute the “9” (the non-zeros).

The number of ways to pick which positions contain the zeros is \binom{n}{i}.

This yields:

\text{Number of strings with } i \text{ zeros} = \binom{n}{i} 9^{n-i} 1^i = \binom{n}{i} 9^{n-i}

The Multinomial Theorem

What if we have a tree that branches more than twice for each decision? The same logic applies, but instead of choosing between two options (x or y) at each node, you choose among three or more options.

Consider partitioning the 10 digits into three distinct buckets:

  • Zero: \{0\} \to 1 possibility (x_1 = 1)

  • Odd digits: \{1, 3, 5, 7, 9\} \to 5 possibilities (x_2 = 5)

  • Even non-zero digits: \{2, 4, 6, 8\} \to 4 possibilities (x_3 = 4)

Notice that 1 + 5 + 4 = 10. The total number of n-digit strings is:10^n = (1 + 5 + 4)^n = (x_1 + x_2 + x_3)^n

Expanding (x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_m)^n means visiting n node. At each node, you must pick exactly one item: an x_1, an x_2, \dots, or an x_m.

If you want a term with:k_1 items of type x_1k_2 items of type x_2\dotsk_m items of type x_mwhere k_1 + k_2 + \dots + k_m = n, the resulting monomial is:

x_1^{k_1} x_2^{k_2} \cdots x_m^{k_m}

To find the coefficient, ask: How many ways can you assign the n positions to these targets?

Choose k_1 positions for type x_1: \binom{n}{k_1} ways. From the remaining n - k_1 spots, choose k_2 positions for type x_2: \binom{n - k_1}{k_2} ways.

From the remaining n - k_1 - k_2 spots, choose k_3 positions for type x_3: \binom{n - k_1 - k_2}{k_3} ways.

Continue until the last group takes all remaining spots. Multiplying these independent choices gives:

\binom{n}{k_1} \binom{n - k_1}{k_2} \cdots = \frac{n!}{k_1!(n - k_1)!} \cdot \frac{(n - k_1)!}{k_2!(n - k_1 - k_2)!} \cdots = \frac{n!}{k_1! k_2! \cdots k_m!}

This is the multinomial coefficient, written as:\binom{n}{k_1, k_2, \dots, k_m} = \frac{n!}{k_1! k_2! \cdots k_m!}

Application to the 3-Category Digit Problem

Suppose you want to know how many n-digit strings contain:

  • Exactly a zeros
  • Exactly b odd digits
  • Exactly c even non-zero digits

where a + b + c = n.

Using the multinomial structure:

Assign the locations: \binom{n}{a, b, c} = \frac{n!}{a!b!c!} ways to decide which positions hold which category.

Fill each category:

  • a spots filled with \{0\}: 1^a ways.
  • b spots filled with odd digits: 5^b ways.
  • c spots filled with even non-zero digits: 4^c ways.

The total count is:

\binom{n}{a, b, c} 1^a 5^b 4^c = \frac{n!}{a!b!c!} 5^b 4^c

Summing this over all non-negative integers where a + b + c = n reconstructs the entire space:

\sum_{a+b+c=n} \binom{n}{a, b, c} 1^a 5^b 4^c = (1 + 5 + 4)^n = 10^n

Just as the Binomial Theorem tracks taking a left or right turn at each step, the Multinomial Theorem tracks navigating an m-way intersection at every step of length n.

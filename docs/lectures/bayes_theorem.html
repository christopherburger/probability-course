6: Bayes’s Theorem

The standard pedagogical introduction to Bayes’ Theorem is perhaps the most destructive in all of mathematics education. Textbooks typically present this monstrosity on day one:

P(A\vert{}B) = \frac{P(B\vert{}A)P(A)}{P(B\vert{}A)P(A) + P(B\vert{}A^c)P(A^c)}

When students see this, they stop thinking probabilistically and start playing algebraic Sudoku. They scan the word problem for numbers, arbitrarily assign them to the variables in the formula, and pray the fraction computes to something between 0 and 1. This guarantees they will fail when attempting Bayesian inference in algorithms later, because they have no idea what the theorem is physically doing.

To fix this, Week 6 must frame Bayes’ Theorem entirely visually. The formula should not be written on the board until the final lecture of the week.

Bayes’ Theorem is Just “Time Travel” on a Tree

You are playing poker. Your opponent makes a massive “all-in” raise (Call this Event B). You need to decide if they are bluffing (Event A).

  1. The Forward Tree (What we know): In Week 5, they learned to build trees chronologically.

    • Node 1: Does the opponent have a good hand or a bad hand (the prior, P(A))?

    • Node 2: Given their hand, do they raise or fold (the conditional, P(B\vert{}A))?

  2. The Crisis: You are sitting at the table. You don’t get to see Node 1. You only see the end result: they raised.

  3. The Bayesian Epiphany: Bayes’ Theorem is just traveling backward up the tree. You know the end result (the raise). You want to know which branch they took to get there.

Phase 2: The “Natural Frequencies” Hack

The human brain is terrible at processing conditional probabilities as percentages, but excellent at processing raw counts. Before using probabilities, teach Bayes using “Natural Frequencies.”

The Scenario: (A classic Mosteller-style setup)

  • 10% of the time, your opponent has a monster hand.

  • 90% of the time, they have garbage.

  • If they have a monster, they raise 80% of the time.

  • If they have garbage, they bluff (raise) 20% of the time.

  • They just raised. What is the probability they have a monster?

The Execution (No Formulas Allowed):

Force the students to pretend they play 100 hands against this opponent.

  1. Split the Universe: Out of 100 hands, 10 are monsters, 90 are garbage.

  2. Calculate the Actions:

    • Of the 10 monsters, they raise 8 times.

    • Of the 90 garbage hands, they bluff 18 times.

  3. Shrink the Universe (Week 5 rules): The opponent raised. The universe of “folds” is dead. The new universe is exactly 8 + 18 = 26 total raises.

  4. The Bayesian Result: Of those 26 raises, 8 came from a monster hand. The probability is 8 / 26 (about 31%).

3: The Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion

Before calculating, we need to identify what is being counted, what is distinguishable, and where overlap or separation occurs. Memorizing formulas is not the goal, these ideas emerge from common structure between similar problems.

Inclusion–Exclusion as Subtraction-Based Compression

A common failure mode in elementary probability is that we learn the inclusion–exclusion formula before we understand why the correction is necessary. The result is symbolic pattern matching: add the sets, subtract an intersection, and hope the signs are right.

What we need is a map of the sample space. We should be able to point to the regions that were counted, identify which regions were counted more than once, and explain how to compensate.

A Continued Example

Consider a standard 52-card deck. Let A be the event that the card is a Heart, and B the event that the card is a Face Card (Jack, Queen, or King). Recall that there are 13 Hearts and 12 Face Cards.

Naive count: 13 + 12 = 25.

Is 25 actually the number of cards satisfying A or B?

No. The Jack, Queen, and King of Hearts belong to both groups, so those three cards were counted twice.

The important observation is not merely that we subtract 3. It is that the same physical outcomes appeared in two different counting categories.

Venn Diagrams

Let us draw two circles labeled A and B that correspond to the events in our continued example.

Place all the events for A and B into their respective circles.

  • Heart: 13

  • Face card: 12

  • Both: 3

Note that the intersection between these events is non-empty, that is, we have overlap between the events. If we count in the naive way by adding up the size of the events, we will have overcounted.

So we will need to remove the overcounting, and we need to approach this systematically. What is the size of the overlap? And how much did we overcount?

The size of the overlap is simply the size of the intersection and how much we overcounted is how many times the intersection was counted. We counted the intersection twice (but only needed to count it once) and so we must reduce our total count by the size of the intersection.

Therefore: 13 + 12 − 3 = 22 cards.

A die is rolled. How many outcomes are even OR greater than 3?

Even outcomes are {2, 4, 6}; outcomes greater than 3 are {4, 5, 6}.

Adding gives 3 + 3 = 6, but 4 and 6 have been counted twice.

The intersection is {4, 6}, containing 2 outcomes.

Thus the union has 6 − 2 = 4 outcomes: {2, 4, 5, 6}.

Extending to Many Sets

Consider a die roll and define:

  • A = even

  • B = greater than 3

  • C = prime

Trying drawing three overlapping circles and physically marking outcomes in each set.

Let us focus only on an outcome in the center—the outcome satisfying A, B, and C. It is initially counted once when A is counted, once when B is counted, and once when C is counted: three copies.

If we subtract the three pairwise intersections, the center outcome loses three copies. But it originally had three copies, so this correction leaves zero copies. We have over-corrected.

Therefore the center must be added back once.

The alternating signs are bookkeeping. For three sets, ‘+ singles − pairwise overlaps + triple overlap’ is a compressed record of how many copies each region currently has and how many copies the correction removes.

A good question to ask is ‘How many times is this region currently being counted, and how many copies do I want?’ That question scales to more complicated counting problems.

From the numbers 1 through 12, count numbers that are divisible by 2 OR 3 OR 5.

Click to show answer

Answer: The single-set counts are 6, 4, and 2.

The pairwise intersections have sizes 2 (divisible by 6), 1 (divisible by 10), and 1 (divisible by 15).

The triple intersection is 0 because no number from 1 to 12 is divisible by 30.

Thus the count is 6 + 4 + 2 − 2 − 1 − 1 + 0 = 8.

Inclusion–exclusion is best viewed as compression. The Venn diagram is the detailed representation; the alternating-sign expression is the compressed representation. If you can construct the map, you can reconstruct the formula.

Stars and Bars

Stars and Bars is often taught as a formula for nonnegative integer solutions. That description is mathematically correct but often too terse to see the generalizability. Another interpretation is a representation theorem: every distribution of identical objects among distinct recipients can be encoded by a string of stars separated into recipient zones.

The poker-chip problem

Suppose a poker table has 10 identical $100 chips and three distinct players: Alice, Bob, and Charlie. Any player may receive zero chips.

The desired outcomes are triples (a,b,c) of nonnegative integers satisfying a + b + c = 10. But rather than immediately write a formula, let us build the representation.

We will use stars to represent the chips and bars for the boundaries.

Line up ten identical chips as stars:

**********

To divide them among three players, only two separators are required:

***|***|****

Everything before the first bar belongs to Alice, everything between the bars belongs to Bob, and everything after the second bar belongs to Charlie.

This example represents (3,3,4). Notice that the players are distinct because their positions in the string are distinct.

Zero allocations are naturally represented:

**********||

This represents (10,0,0). The adjacent bars create an empty middle region, so Bob gets zero.

Repeated-letter Anagrams Revisted

We can think of a stars-and-bars distribution as a restricted anagram problem.

There are 10 identical stars and 2 identical bars, giving 12 total positions. Choose which 2 positions contain bars; all remaining positions contain stars.

Number of distributions = \binom{12}{2} = 66.

Equivalently, the repeated-character calculation is \frac{12!}{10!2!}.

How many ways are there to distribute 8 identical tokens among Alice, Bob, Charlie, and Dana, allowing zero?

Click to show answer

Answer: Four recipients require three bars. The representation contains 8 stars and 3 bars, for 11 positions. Choose the three bar positions: \binom{11}{3} = 165.

Note that number of bars is determined by the number of boundaries needed to create the recipient zones: k recipients require k−1 bars.

The Formula

For n identical objects distributed among k distinct recipients with zero allowed, there are n stars and k−1 bars, giving n+k−1 total positions. Choosing the bar positions gives:

\binom{n + k - 1}{k - 1}

The Physical Nature of the Objects Changes the Mathematics

The physical identity of the objects determines what constitutes a different outcome. Changing identical keys into distinct keys does not merely change a number in the formula; it changes the sample-space architecture.

The casino hotel-key problem

A casino manager is handing out 5 complimentary hotel room keys to 3 distinct high rollers. Compare two versions.

Five identical standard-room keys

Assume the five keys are interchangeable: giving Alice key 1 and Bob key 2 is not meaningfully different from giving Alice key 2 and Bob key 1.

Only the number of keys received by each person matters. Let (a,b,c) be the allocations. Then a+b+c=5 with a,b,c ≥ 0. This is a stars-and-bars problem with 5 stars and 2 bars, so the state-space size is \binom{7}{2} 21.

Note here that the physical objects are indistinguishable. A person’s allocation is summarized completely by a count.

Five uniquely themed penthouse keys

Assume the five keys correspond to five distinct suites: Roman, Egyptian, Art Deco, Presidential, and Ocean. A particular key can be assigned to any one of the three players.

Each of the 5 distinct keys has 3 possible recipients. Therefore the number of assignments is 3^5 = 243.

Note that the objects are distinguishable. Which player receives which particular key matters.

Why this is a different mathematical architecture

Feature Identical keys Distinct keys
What matters? How many each player gets Which key each player gets
Object identity Irrelevant Relevant
Representation Stars + bars 5 independent key assignments
Typical structure Integer solutions Functions / assignments
State-space size \binom{7}{2} = 21 3^5 = 243

A useful question: If I swap two objects between recipients, does the outcome change?

If NO, the objects may be indistinguishable for this problem. If YES, the objects are distinguishable and a different counting architecture is likely required.

Five identical $100 chips are distributed among three players, but every player must receive at least one. How many possible distributions are there?

Hint: It can be easier to handle constraints by modifying the physical model before counting.

Answer: Give each player one chip first. Three chips have now been assigned, leaving 2 identical chips to distribute freely among 3 players. Thus \binom{2+3-1}{3-1} = \binom{4}{2} = 6.

If 8 identical objects are to be divided among 4 bins, how many divisions are possible?

How many divisions are possible if each bin must contain at least one object?

Answer: With 8 objects and 4 bins, we can view this as having 11 total physical objects. We only then need to choose where the dividing points that indicate the bins go.

This gives us \binom{11}{3}.

If there must be at least one object in each bin, then we already have four objects allocated as there are four bins. We can then view our total number of objects as 11 - 4 = 7 and this then gives us \binom{7}{3}.
